# Why each piece exists

Every file in this kit is there because leaving it out causes a specific, predictable problem. Here's the problem each one prevents.

## The source file

**Without it:** The AI answers from whatever it was trained on, plus whatever you pasted last week, plus a vague memory of your website. It sounds confident. It's wrong about the price. Nobody can say where the wrong price came from, so nobody can fix it at the root.

**With it:** There's one place facts live. When the price changes, one person edits one file. The next draft is right. The old drafts can be found by source version.

**The part people skip:** The owner's name. A source file without a name on it is a file that drifts. Six months in, nobody knows if it's current, so everyone checks around it, and now you have two sources again.

## The request form

**Without it:** "Write something about SOC 2." The AI writes something. You don't like it. It writes something else. Four rounds later you've got a piece and no idea whether it's the right one, because nobody ever said what right was.

**With it:** The "Point" line is a sentence you can hold a draft up against. If the draft makes that point, it's done. If it doesn't, you know what to say.

**The part people skip:** "Must not say." It feels negative. It's the most useful section. The source file's "don't" lines go here, and so do the things that are fine in general but wrong for this piece.

## The tags

**Without them:** A draft is a wall of fluent prose. The true sentences and the invented sentences look identical. Checking it means re-researching everything, which nobody does, so nobody checks.

**With them:** You read the `[?]` list first. That's where the risk is. The `[S]` facts you spot-check. The whole review takes five minutes because the AI already sorted the sentences into "look here" and "probably fine."

**The part people skip:** Spot-checking the `[S]` tags. The AI is usually right. "Usually" is the word that gets the wrong quote published.

## The checklist

**Without it:** Review is a feeling. Somebody reads the draft, feels okay about it, and it goes out. Or three people read it, each feels differently, and it sits for a week while they reconcile their feelings.

**With it:** Five questions, yes or no, one person. It's done when it's done, and "done" has a definition.

**The part people skip:** Question 5. "Would you put your name on it." That's the only question that can't be gamed. If the answer is a hesitation, the hesitation is the review note.

## The log

**Without it:** "How long does content take?" "Depends." "Who approved the post with the wrong price?" "I think it was... let me check Slack."

**With it:** The dates are in a table. Average them. The names are in a table. Look them up. After twenty rows you'll know something true about your own process that you didn't know before, and it'll usually be that the waiting is longer than the working.

**The part people skip:** Filling it in. It takes thirty seconds. Put it in the checklist's "what to do with the answers" step, which is why it's there.

## The one person who checks

**Without it:** The person who asked for the piece checks the piece. Every question comes back yes, because they wanted it to be yes before the draft existed.

**With it:** Someone else, with a name, with five minutes. That's the whole governance model. It scales to five gates and two auditors in the full pipeline, but it starts with one person who isn't the requester.
